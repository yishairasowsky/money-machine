## 2026-08-27 — added Track E (options-income-sim) and Track F (tutoring-smoke-test)

Built a stdlib-only covered-call ("the wheel", simplified) simulator against a synthetic price path, ran three scenarios (uptrend/flat/downtrend) with identical seed/vol/strike so only market direction varies. It reproduced the textbook trade-off honestly: in a +20%/yr uptrend, covered calls returned +43.37% vs. +48.14% buy-and-hold (underperformed by 4.78 pts — capped upside cost more than the 28.82%-of-equity premium collected); in flat and −15%/yr downtrend markets, the same premium income turned a loss/near-flat market into a small win (beat buy-and-hold by 1.78 and 4.19 pts respectively). Worth noting: the first parameter choice (5% OTM, 25% IV) made premium dominate in *all* scenarios including the uptrend, which didn't match how covered calls actually behave — the numbers above are after correcting to more realistic assumptions (8% OTM, 20% IV). A reminder that a backtest's conclusion can be an artifact of unrealistic inputs, not the strategy.

Also built a demand smoke-test for high-school math tutoring: a landing page plus one real, correctness-checked sample lesson (factoring quadratics — the 3 mistakes that cause most errors) as the actual value-proof, not just marketing copy. Flagged explicitly that this track is economically different from the rest: it's an hours-for-money service, not something that gets more passive with scale unless later turned into a group/course format — though it can pay sooner than the others while a real audience/track record doesn't exist yet.

## 2026-08-28 — options-income-sim: added the actual full wheel

The track was previously mislabeled as "the wheel, simplified" but only implemented the covered-call leg (always holding shares). Added `--strategy wheel`, the real full wheel: alternates cash-secured puts (while holding cash) and covered calls (while holding shares) as assignment flips it between phases. `--strategy covered-call` stays the default, output byte-for-byte compatible with before.

Honest finding, same default parameters as the existing covered-call table: the wheel is not strictly better. It beats buy-and-hold by more than covered-calls-only in the downtrend (-0.66% vs -22.25%, both vs buy-and-hold's -26.44%) but underperforms by far more in the uptrend (+11.43% vs covered-calls' +43.37%, against buy-and-hold's +48.14%) — a 36.72-point gap, roughly 8x covered-calls' 4.78-point gap in the same scenario. The reason: the wheel spends real time in its put phase holding cash instead of shares, earning no market exposure while buy-and-hold stays fully invested. Neither strategy dominates the other; the wheel trades uptrend upside for downside protection.

## 2026-08-29 — invest-backtester: multi-seed robustness test closes the "one path" gap

Every finding in Track A so far came from a single synthetic price path — flagged repeatedly in SCORING.md as the track's weakest evidence ("two runs, one synthetic path each"). Added `robustness_test.py`: runs both SMA crossover and RSI mean-reversion against buy-and-hold on 30 independent synthetic paths (seeds 0-29, same seeded-random-walk generator as `options-income-sim`) instead of just one, and reports win rate and average excess return across all of them.

With a realistic +8%/yr drift and 25% annual volatility: SMA crossover beat buy-and-hold on only 6/30 paths (20%), averaging -15.86 points. RSI mean-reversion beat it more often — 13/30 (43%) — but with a *worse* average (-23.01 pts), because a handful of paths were catastrophic (worst case -172.81 pts): RSI's oversold signal firing right before a price kept falling anyway, a real and known mean-reversion failure mode, not a fluke. In a flat 0%-drift market both strategies still lose on average, with win rates closer to a coin flip. This confirms the original single-path result was the typical case, not bad luck — real, new evidence, not just more prototyping.

## 2026-08-30 — options-income-sim: same robustness test, sharper finding

Applied the same fix to Track E that Track A got yesterday: `robustness_test.py` runs covered-calls and the full wheel against buy-and-hold on 30 independent synthetic paths per scenario (uptrend/flat/downtrend) instead of the README's single seed=7 run each.

The result confirmed the original direction but surfaced something the one-seed version couldn't see: covered calls' uptrend outcome is right-skewed. It actually *beats* buy-and-hold on the majority of paths (17/30, 57%; median +3.55 pts) — small, steady wins most of the time — but the *average* is negative (-4.93 pts) because a minority of paths are catastrophic (worst: -97.24 pts) where the underlying ran hard and capped upside gave back far more than premium ever collected. The wheel shows the same shape, far more extreme: only 7/30 (23%) uptrend wins, -43.98 avg pts, worst path -225.12. Flat and downtrend scenarios stayed favorable for both strategies (83-97% win rates), and the wheel's downtrend edge over covered-calls-only held up robustly across paths (+18.58 vs +10.10 avg excess pts), not just on the one seed originally reported. Win rate and average return told two different stories on the same uptrend data — a reminder that "did it beat the benchmark?" and "what's the expected value?" can disagree, and checking only one can hide a fat tail.

## 2026-08-31 — real-estate-analyzer: multi-year Monte Carlo, a harsher test than the sensitivity grid

Same underlying instinct as the last two days' robustness tests, adapted to this track's actual structure: `--sensitivity` only ever asked whether a deal's *Year-1* verdict was fragile to a different price or rate quote. Added `monte_carlo.py`: simulates 2,000 independent 10-year holds per deal with random rent growth (~3%/yr), random expense inflation (~3%/yr), and a 12% per-year independent chance of one surprise repair costing 1-3 months' rent — the real risk this track's own README already names ("maintenance-as-%-of-rent is a rule of thumb, not a bid") but had never actually modeled.

Honest finding: the "good" Maple Street deal never has a single negative-cash-flow year across all 2,000 ten-year trials (median cumulative cash flow $123k). The "marginal" Oakwood Ave deal — which *passed* its Year-1 sensitivity check well enough to only flip negative at +0.75pp on rate — has at least one negative-cash-flow year in **65.6%** of 10-year trials, and its median worst single year is itself negative (-$2,094), from ordinary variance alone, no bad luck required. The "bad" Riverside Condo has a negative year in 100% of trials, as expected. The takeaway: a deal can pass a Year-1 snapshot test and still fail a realistic multi-year hold — these are genuinely different questions, and "marginal" here answers them very differently.

## 2026-09-01 — options-income-sim: OTM% strike-distance sensitivity

Three straight days of robustness/Monte Carlo passes (A, E, D) all held their strategy's parameters fixed and varied the random seed. Today's angle is different: `otm_sensitivity.py` sweeps the one parameter a real covered-call seller actually chooses each period — how far out-of-the-money the strike is — from 2% to 20%, 25 seeds per point, in the uptrend and downtrend scenarios where the trade-off is sharpest.

Honest finding: there is no OTM% in the swept range that both beats buy-and-hold on average in an uptrend *and* keeps a real downside cushion. Selling farther out of the money almost erases the uptrend drag (win rate climbs 20%→88%, average underperformance shrinks from -27.96 to -0.46 pts as OTM% rises from 2% to 20%) — but the same move erases the premium income that is the entire reason to sell calls in the first place (60.21% of equity collected at 2% OTM vs. 0.18% at 20% OTM), so a strike wide enough to stop dragging on a rally is, by the same mechanism, too far out to meaningfully cushion a downtrend. The 8% default sits in the middle of this trade-off, not above it — a covered-call seller isn't picking a "good" strike, they're picking which side of this trade-off to be on.

## 2026-09-02 — invest-backtester: window-pair sensitivity closes the "bad parameter" gap

Yesterday's OTM-strike sweep on options-income-sim asked whether that track's one real seller decision (strike distance) changed the verdict. Today's applies the same idea to Track A: `window_sensitivity.py` sweeps five classic SMA fast/slow window pairs (10/30, 20/50, 10/50, 20/100, 50/200) across 30 seeds each in three market regimes, instead of holding the 20/50 default fixed like `robustness_test.py` did.

Honest finding: no window pair changes the story. Every pair loses to buy-and-hold on the large majority of uptrend paths (13-23% win rate, all strongly negative average excess) and beats it on the majority of downtrend paths (63-70% win rate, all positive average) — the spread between window pairs is a few points, dwarfed by the 30-40+ point gap between regimes. The 20/50 default wasn't an unlucky choice that made SMA crossover look worse than it is; the underlying mechanism (lag: the crossover confirms a move only after it's underway) doesn't go away with a different window length. Between `robustness_test.py` (rules out "unlucky seed") and this (rules out "bad parameter"), Track A's core finding is now on considerably firmer ground than the original single-path, single-window result.

## 2026-09-03 — real-estate-analyzer: down-payment (leverage) sensitivity

Same pattern as the strike-distance sweep on options-income-sim (9/1) and the window-pair sweep on invest-backtester (9/2), applied to the third quant track: `leverage_sensitivity.py` sweeps the one input that's actually the buyer's own decision -- down payment % -- across 10% to 40%, rerunning the 10-year Monte Carlo survivability check (1000 trials/point) at each level for all three sample deals.

Honest finding: leverage matters a lot for two of the three deals and not at all for the third, in a genuinely diagnostic way. The "good" Maple Street deal isn't unconditionally safe -- at 10% down (higher cash-on-cash, 16.77% vs. 12.86% at its own 20%) the negative-year rate jumps to 9.3% from near-zero. The "marginal" Oakwood deal improves substantially with more down payment (66.6% → 18.2% negative-year risk from 20% to 40% down) but never gets comfortable even at the least-leveraged option tested. The "bad" Riverside Condo stays at 100% negative-year risk at every down-payment level from 10% to 40% -- because its problem is the price-to-rent ratio itself, not the financing structure, no down payment size fixes it. That third result is the most useful one in practice: it's the difference between a deal where putting more cash down is the right move and one where it's wasted money on a deal that was never going to work.
