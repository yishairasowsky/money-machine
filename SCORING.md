# Cross-track scoring

A systematic, honest comparison of all six prototypes — so "which one has potential" is answered from evidence gathered so far, not a vibe. Every score is 1 (worst) to 5 (best) for that dimension; higher isn't automatically "better track," since the dimensions trade off against each other (e.g. tutoring pays fastest but scales worst).

## What this actually says, read honestly

- **Nothing here has touched a real customer, a real trade, or a real dollar yet.** Every "confidence" score above is about the prototype's internal logic, not market validation — that's a hard limit of this repo, not something more prototyping fixes.
- **Fastest plausible real dollar:** tutoring (F) or the SaaS landing page (C) — both just need a human to actually post them somewhere and see what happens. This is the cheapest way to convert "which track has potential" from a guess into evidence.
- **Most capital-gated:** real estate (D) and options (E) — both need real money committed before you get a real signal, which is exactly why they were built as paper tools first.
- **Most likely to disappoint on pure math:** the backtester (A) — both SMA crossover and RSI mean-reversion now lose to a coin-flip-simple benchmark (buy-and-hold) on *most* of 30 independent synthetic paths (20% and 43% win rates respectively), not just the one path originally tested — consistent with a large body of real evidence against simple technical-indicator strategies.
- **Sharpest new signal:** real estate (D)'s sensitivity analysis — a deal's headline cash-on-cash number can hide how little room it has for error. The "good" deal earned its label partly by staying good across a realistic range of rates, not just at one assumed number; the "marginal" deal's positive cash flow was never robust in the first place. Worth checking any real deal this way before trusting a single-point projection. Now confirmed over time too: a 10-year/2000-trial Monte Carlo shows the "marginal" deal hits a negative-cash-flow year in 65.6% of simulated holds, purely from ordinary rent/expense variance and the occasional surprise repair — a real multi-year hold is a much harsher test than a Year-1 snapshot passing its sensitivity check.
- **Most counterintuitive finding:** options (E)'s full wheel is not an upgrade on covered-calls-only, it's a different bet — much better crash protection, much worse in a strong rally, because it spends time out of the market entirely while holding cash. "The wheel" gets talked about online like strictly-better income farming; this repo's own numbers say otherwise, and that holds up across 30 independent paths, not just one.
- **Most misleading-if-you-only-look-at-one-number:** options (E)'s uptrend covered-call result — it *beats* buy-and-hold on the majority of paths (57%, median +3.55 pts) but has a *negative* average (-4.93 pts) because a minority of paths are catastrophic (worst -97.24 pts). Win rate and average return told two different stories on the same data; checking both would have caught something a single "did it beat buy-and-hold?" run wouldn't.
- **The one real lever a covered-call seller has, quantified:** options (E)'s strike-distance sweep shows OTM% isn't a market fact, it's the seller's own choice — and it's a real trade-off, not a dial you can tune your way out of. Going farther OTM raises the uptrend win rate from 20% to 88% across the swept range, but the same move drops premium income (the actual downside cushion) from 60% of equity to under 1%. There is no strike distance in the swept range that keeps both benefits at once.
- **Best next move for actual evidence, not more building:** post the tutoring landing page and the SaaS landing page somewhere real (they're both nearly free to test) before spending more engineering time on any track. Prototyping further without that step just produces more untested opinions.
