# How much house can I actually afford?

A lender will tell you the maximum they'll approve you for. That number is not the same as what you can actually afford — it's usually higher.

**Use the 28/36 rule as a ceiling, not a target.** Your total housing payment (principal, interest, property tax, home insurance, and HOA if any) shouldn't exceed 28% of gross monthly income. All debt payments combined — housing plus car loans, student loans, credit cards — shouldn't exceed 36%. On $80,000/year gross income (~$6,667/month), that's roughly $1,867/month max for housing alone. Many lenders will approve you up to 43-45% back-end, especially on FHA loans — that's the max they'll allow, not what's comfortable.

**The interest rate moves affordability more than the price tag does.** A $400,000 loan over 30 years at 7% runs roughly $2,660/month in principal and interest; the same loan at 6% runs roughly $2,400/month — about $260/month, or close to $95,000, over the life of the loan, from one percentage point. Get a real rate quote before you start house-hunting off a number you saw online.

**Put 20% down if you can, to skip PMI.** Below 20% down, most lenders require private mortgage insurance — typically 0.5-1.5% of the loan balance per year — until you build enough equity to cancel it. On a $320,000 loan that's roughly $130-400/month added to your payment for no equity benefit.

**Budget for the costs beyond the mortgage payment.** Closing costs typically run 2-5% of the purchase price, due upfront. After move-in, budget 1-2% of the home's value per year for maintenance — a cost renters never see and first-time buyers consistently underestimate.

**Next action:** take your actual gross monthly income, multiply by 0.28, and treat that as your real housing payment ceiling — then get a pre-approval and compare the lender's number against it before you fall in love with a listing above it.
