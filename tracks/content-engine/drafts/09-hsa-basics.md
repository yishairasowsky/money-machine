# How does an HSA work, and is it worth it?

Most people treat their HSA like a medical debit card. Used differently, it's arguably the single best tax-advantaged account available to anyone who qualifies — better than a 401(k) or Roth IRA on paper, for one specific reason.

**The mechanism, and why it beats the other accounts:** an HSA (Health Savings Account) is available if you're enrolled in a qualifying high-deductible health plan (HDHP). It offers a triple tax advantage — contributions go in pre-tax (or are tax-deductible), the balance grows tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses come out tax-free. A 401(k) or traditional IRA gives you two of those three; a Roth gives you a different two. The HSA is the only account that gives all three.

**Unlike an FSA, it doesn't expire.** Flexible spending accounts force you to spend the balance by year-end or lose it. HSA balances roll over indefinitely and stay yours even if you change jobs or health plans.

**The strategy that makes it powerful: pay medical costs out of pocket now, invest the HSA instead.** Most HSA providers let you invest the balance above a small cash cushion (often $1,000-$2,000, varies by provider) in mutual funds or ETFs, similar to a 401(k). If you can afford to pay current medical bills out of pocket and keep the receipts, you can let the HSA balance invest and compound for decades, then reimburse yourself tax-free at any point in the future — there's no deadline on that reimbursement.

**A concrete number:** contributing roughly $3,000/year and investing it at an assumed 7% average annual return, after 20 years you'd have contributed $60,000 and the account would be worth roughly $123,000 — about $63,000 of growth that's never taxed if spent on qualified medical expenses (which, over a lifetime, most people eventually have).

**After age 65, it loosens further:** you can withdraw for any reason, not just medical, without the usual 20% penalty — non-medical withdrawals are simply taxed as ordinary income at that point, functioning like a traditional IRA. Before 65, non-medical withdrawals face both income tax and a 20% penalty, so this only works as a strategy if you keep it for genuine future medical spending or wait until 65.

**Next action:** check if you're currently enrolled in an HDHP and eligible to contribute; if so, and you can afford to pay routine medical costs out of pocket, move your HSA balance above the provider's minimum cash cushion into low-cost index funds instead of leaving it sitting as cash.
