# Should I pay off my car loan early or invest that money instead?

This only becomes a real question once your higher-interest debt is gone and you're capturing any employer 401(k) match — those two beat both options below. If that's already true, here's how to think about the car loan specifically.

**Compare your loan's APR to a conservative long-run market return assumption, not to nothing.** Paying down debt early earns you a guaranteed return equal to the interest rate you stop paying. Investing instead earns an uncertain return that has historically averaged somewhere around 7% annually for a diversified stock index over long periods, but with real down years included. If your APR is meaningfully above that (say, 7%+), paying it off is the safer and often better move. If your APR is well below it — common with 0-4% manufacturer financing promotions — investing tends to win in expectation, but you're trading a sure thing for a probable one.

**A concrete comparison:** $12,000 auto loan balance, 3% APR, 3 years (36 months) remaining, and $200/month you could put toward either option.
- Extra payments toward the loan pay it off faster and save roughly $300-400 in interest you'd otherwise have paid over the remaining term (the exact figure depends on the amortization schedule — check yours with a loan calculator).
- That same $200/month invested for 36 months at an assumed 7% average annual return grows to roughly $8,000, against $7,200 contributed — about $800 of growth.
In this example, investing wins by a meaningful margin in expectation, but the loan payoff is the only one of the two numbers that's guaranteed.

**Don't skip the emergency fund to do either.** Extra cash toward a low-rate loan or an investment account is money you can't get back quickly if your car breaks down or you lose income — both of these come after a starter emergency fund, not before.

**The behavioral case for paying it off anyway:** a paid-off car loan is one fewer required monthly payment, which lowers your fixed costs and your stress if income gets disrupted — a real value that the math above doesn't capture in dollars.

**Next action:** pull your loan's exact current APR from your statement or servicer portal, compare it to roughly 6-7%, and route extra money toward whichever side of that line you're on — loan payoff if above it, an investment account if meaningfully below it and your emergency fund and higher-rate debt are already handled.
