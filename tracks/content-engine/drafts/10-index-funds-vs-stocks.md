# Index funds vs. individual stocks: what should a total beginner actually buy?

Picking individual stocks feels like the "real" way to invest. For most beginners, it's the more expensive way to learn a lesson an index fund teaches for free.

**What each actually is:** an index fund is a single fund holding hundreds or thousands of companies at once — an S&P 500 fund holds roughly 500 of the largest US companies, a total market fund holds thousands. Buying one share gives you instant diversification. Buying an individual stock is a concentrated bet on one company's future — no diversification unless you buy many of them and manage that mix yourself.

**The diversification math is stark.** Put $10,000 into a single stock and that company has a bad year — a 50% drop, which even large, established companies have done — and you've lost $5,000. Put the same $10,000 into a broad index fund and one company in it collapsing, even to zero, costs you a fraction of a percent of your total, because no single holding is more than a small slice of the fund.

**Fees are low and the comparison isn't close.** Broad, low-cost index funds commonly charge expense ratios around 0.03-0.10% annually — roughly $3-10/year per $10,000 invested. Picking your own stocks has no fund fee, but real costs show up as time spent researching, concentration risk, and the well-documented tendency for individual investors to buy after a stock has already risen and sell after it's already dropped.

**Most professional stock-pickers don't beat the index either.** Independent studies comparing actively managed funds to their benchmark index have repeatedly found that a large majority of actively managed large-cap funds underperform a simple S&P 500 index over 10-15 year periods — professionals with research teams, not beating a fund that just buys the whole market. That's a strong signal about the odds a beginner picking individual stocks alone is facing.

**This isn't "never buy individual stocks" — it's sequencing.** For money you can't afford to lose or will need within 5 years, neither belongs in stocks at all; that money belongs in cash or a high-yield savings account. For long-term money (retirement, 10+ years out), a low-cost broad index fund should be the core holding. Individual stocks, if you want them, come later, with a small amount you can genuinely afford to lose, once you understand what you're buying.

**Next action:** if you don't already hold a low-cost, broad index fund (S&P 500 or total market) as the core of your long-term investing account, open one this month and automate a contribution — treat individual stock picks, if any, as a small side allocation, not your starting point.
